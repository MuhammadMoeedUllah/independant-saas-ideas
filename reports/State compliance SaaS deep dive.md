# Evidence reshapes four state-compliance SaaS bets

None of the four ideas holds up as pitched, but two are worth building once reshaped. The **California DROP engine (idea 1)** has the most certain demand. Roughly 600–650 registered data brokers must each process a hashed deletion list covering more than 500,000 Californians every 45 days. Independent audits begin in 2028 and look back to August 1, 2026. But **DropClerk already sells the basic filer for $99 a month**, and enterprise privacy platforms bundle the same function, so the opening is an audit-evidence and suppression-operations product for small brokers, with a California ceiling of roughly $3–7M ARR. The **pay-transparency scanner (idea 2)** rests on a premise that Washington's 2025 amendment and guidance in Colorado, Illinois and New York have largely voided: employers are generally **not liable for scraped reposts**. It survives only as a cure-deadline and evidence tool for employers exposed to private suits in Washington, Virginia and Connecticut. The **PFAS network (idea 4)** has the most open competitive space and the longest runway, with near-universal bans arriving in 2032, but it faces a two-sided cold start, unverified rights to share lab reports, and repeated state delays. It should launch as a rules engine plus checkout block. The **EPR simulator (idea 5)** is the weakest: at least six free calculators already model fees and redesigns, only Oregon and Colorado invoice today, and no evidence shows converters will pay. Ranked on the scoring below: **DROP first, pay transparency second, PFAS third (highest upside, highest risk), EPR fourth.** Every figure comes from search-engine summaries rather than full primary pages, so verify the load-bearing numbers before they go into a pitch or a go/no-go memo.

## Idea 1: the DROP filer is crowded, the audit-evidence lane is not

Since **August 1, 2026**, every California-registered data broker has had to access the Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) at least once every 45 days and download its SHA-256-hashed deletion lists. The broker standardizes and hashes its own records to find matches, then reports one of four statuses for every request within 45 days: deleted, opted out, exempt or not found. It must also direct service providers and contractors to delete matching data, and keep a suppression list that screens newly acquired data ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/californias-new-delete-request-tool-impacts-data-brokers-and-residents); [Troutman](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/12/analyzing-the-california-delete-act-regulations/)). Failure to delete costs **$200 per request per day** ([Troutman fact sheet](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/941/2026/03/Troutman_PrivacyCyber_CADeleteAct_Final.pdf)). Every enrolled consumer appears on the lists a broker selects, so even a broker that holds data on few of them must file a status for hundreds of thousands of identifiers.

The statute adds an **independent audit from January 1, 2028**, repeated every three years, with reports kept for six years ([SB 361 text via PUBINFO mirror](https://github.com/aaronjuar-ez/PUBINFO-2025)). The draft audit rules, in formal rulemaking since August 2026, bar internal auditors, set no size threshold and forbid findings based mainly on attestations. They also require auditors to independently verify nine components, from list selection through hashing, matching, suppression and service-provider deletion. Law-firm summaries put the first reports at **November 1, 2028, covering activity from August 1, 2026** ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/a-fresh-take/drop-is-live-what-data-brokers-need-to-know-as-calprivacy-ramps-up-oversight-102nqhf); [WSGR](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/calprivacy-authorizes-rulemaking-on-opt-out-signals-raises-data-broker-fees-and-starts-the-clock-on-drop-amendment-comment-period.html)), while Clark Hill reports January 1, 2029 ([Clark Hill](https://www.clarkhill.com/news-events/news/is-your-business-a-data-broker-californias-drop-goes-live-and-calprivacy-continues-to-enforce-delete-act/)). The annual registration fee rises from **$6,000 to $9,500 for 2027** ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike)).

### DataGrail at the top, a $99 DropClerk at the bottom

Within months of the deadline, the broker-side market split into two tiers. At the top, enterprise privacy platforms bundle DROP processing. DataGrail sells a dedicated "fully automated" module that downloads lists, matches hash-to-hash across connected systems, executes deletions and files statuses, and claims raw identifiers never change hands ([DataGrail](https://www.datagrail.io/solutions/drop-compliance/)). Transcend ingests DROP files by CSV or API and runs downstream erasure ([Transcend](https://transcend.io/blog/calprivacy-drop-compliance-guide)). Ketch automates deletion across connected systems and generates the audit trail ([Ketch](https://www.ketch.com/blog/posts/california-drop-platform-data-broker-compliance)). TrustArc submits the list to its rights-request manager as "a single, auditable request" ([TrustArc](https://trustarc.com/resource/california-delete-act-drop-platform-data-brokers/)). These platforms cost real money: Vendr purchase data puts small and mid-market DataGrail deployments at **$30,000–60,000 a year, with a median of $50,000 across 71 purchases** ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/datagrail)). That prices out most small brokers.

At the bottom sits **DropClerk**. It hashes and matches records in the broker's own browser, returns only the state's work-item IDs with a status digit, and charges **$99 a month per registration, with no tiers and no contract** ([DropClerk](https://dropclerk.com/)). Beside it are Drop Privacy, DROP Autopilot (which already markets 2028 audit preparation) and Optacy ([Optacy](https://optacy.com/product/the-california-drop-act/)). UnsubCentral, the email suppression-list incumbent, pitches itself as DROP matching and suppression infrastructure ([UnsubCentral](https://www.unsubcentral.com/california-drop-and-the-delete-act-operational-guide-for-data-brokers/)). Free hashing kits sit on GitHub, though the most complete one notes that it lacks Greek and Cyrillic name transliteration, a gap that would silently miss matches ([Ozvel/california-DROP](https://github.com/Ozvel/california-DROP)). No funding, team or customer counts surfaced for any of the small entrants, and Osano, BigID and LiveRamp were never checked.

### A product brokers must buy, in a market of roughly $3–7M

Demand is not the question, and the regulator publishes the lead list. CalPrivacy's registry CSV gives each broker's website, contact email and prior-year request metrics ([CPPA registry](https://cppa.ca.gov/data_broker_registry/)). A researcher's analysis of the September 1 snapshot found that **49% of brokers received fewer than 100 deletion requests in 2024 and 69% fewer than 1,000**, while 120 received 10,000 or more ([delistmydata](https://github.com/delistmydata/ca-data-broker-registry)). That leaves roughly 400–450 small firms with no request-handling tooling built for this volume, now facing hundreds of thousands of hashed identifiers on a 45-day clock. Two-thirds of registrants declare no sensitive data, which fits B2B-contact and lead-generation vendors ([BrokerBlitzer](https://brokerblitzer.com/registry)), the cost-sensitive buyers already absorbing a 58% fee increase.

Enforcement is steady but modest. CalPrivacy's strike force has mostly settled failure-to-register cases, at **$36,400 (SalesIntel) to $116,490 (LocateSmarter)**, and its orders now require DROP processing and published metrics ([CalPrivacy, SalesIntel](https://privacy.ca.gov/2026/09/calprivacy-continues-enforcement-blitz-with-action-against-virginia-data-broker/); [CalPrivacy, LocateSmarter](https://privacy.ca.gov/2026/08/calprivacy-brings-first-action-against-a-data-broker-under-both-the-ccpa-and-delete-act/)). No action for failing to process DROP deletions had surfaced by October 9. A September advisory adds a $200-a-day exposure for inaccurate registry information ([CalPrivacy advisory](https://privacy.ca.gov/2026/09/enforcement-advisory-targets-incorrect-information-in-data-broker-registration/)).

The ceiling is the problem. At DropClerk's price, every one of 600 registrants adds up to about **$0.7M ARR**. At $6,000–15,000 a year for a richer product, 450 small brokers yield **roughly $3–7M ARR** in California. That is a sound bootstrapped business and a weak venture one.

Upside depends on other states adopting DROP-style systems. Connecticut's Public Act 26-64 reportedly creates a second deletion portal by July 1, 2028, with brokers polling it every 45 days from October 2028, but that rests on a single secondary guide ([Pandectes](https://pandectes.io/blog/data-broker-compliance-in-2026-a-guide-to-california-connecticut-new-jersey-and-beyond/)). Vermont cut its universal deletion mechanism down to a study ([Privacy Daily](https://privacy-daily.com/article/2026/05/29/vt-data-broker-bill-goes-to-the-governor-after-losing-right-to-delete-2605290056?BC=bc_6a1a6531e2be3)). New Jersey's new broker law has no deletion mechanism, and New York's bill is stalled.

Three downside risks are concrete. Consumer outreach ran on a one-time $7.9M allocation, so sign-up growth slows if it is not renewed ([Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/privacy-and-data-security/most-california-data-brokers-saw-few-privacy-opt-out-requests)). The core engine is easy to copy. And brokers have a documented habit of under-investing: in a UC Irvine study, more than 40% of 543 brokers never answered access requests ([arXiv 2506.21914](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.21914)).

### Three API calls and exact hashing make the build small but unforgiving

The build is small and unusually well specified. DROP's REST API has three operations (download a ZIP of CSVs, upload status CSVs, amend) plus a manual portal path. Brokers receive only SHA-256/Base64 hashes across six list types: name+DOB+ZIP, email, phone, mobile ad ID, name+VIN and connected-TV ID. They never see raw personal data ([CalPrivacy OpenAPI v1.2.0, verbatim mirror](https://github.com/api-evangelist/california-privacy-protection-agency)).

The difficulty is precision. The final regulations replaced a proposed 50%-of-identifiers fuzzy match with a **100% match standard** ([FSOR](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/drop_fsor_15day.pdf)), and production validates only file format. A broker that handles leading ZIP zeros or email whitespace differently from the spec therefore silently reports "not found" and looks compliant. A 409 response blocks any new download until every work item in the open batch has a status, so a pipeline that drops rows locks the broker out and starts the violation clock. The documentation also changed three times between April and July 2026, including a ZIP+4 rule change, so the hashing library needs versioning.

The researchers estimate **10–14 engineer-weeks** for a credible MVP. That covers a DROP client and a hashing library with a conformance suite. It covers connectors that hash data inside the broker's own Postgres, Snowflake, BigQuery or CSV stores. And it covers matching with exemption tagging, deletion and service-provider notice workflows, a persistent suppression store, a hash-chained audit log retained for six years, and a metrics reporter.

One architecture question is unresolved and decides the design. TrustArc states that third-party platforms "are not permitted to access DROP directly on a broker's behalf" ([TrustArc](https://trustarc.com/resource/california-delete-act-drop-platform-data-brokers/)), yet DataGrail and Ketch market automated processing. Until CalPrivacy clarifies, the safe design keeps the broker's API key and raw data inside the broker's own environment, the same choice DropClerk made.

### Reshape as audit evidence and launch before the January 2027 renewal

The evidence turns idea 1 from a "DROP fulfillment engine" into **DROP compliance operations and audit evidence for small brokers**. Do not compete on the 45-day filer; DropClerk has anchored it at $99. Compete on what the 2028 audit will test and a $99 browser tool does not cover. Start with a hashing conformance check that answers "are you silently reporting not-found?" Add suppression screening at ingest, which proposed amendments would extend to every prior requester, including people who never matched ([WSGR](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/calprivacy-authorizes-rulemaking-on-opt-out-signals-raises-data-broker-fees-and-starts-the-clock-on-drop-amendment-comment-period.html)). Then add service-provider deletion notices with acknowledgements, management of registry accuracy and SB 361 disclosures, the July 1 metrics report, and an evidence pack organized around the nine audit components, retained from August 2026 onward.

Price it as flat annual tiers of roughly **$6,000–15,000**. That sits below DataGrail, above DropClerk, and small next to a $9,500 fee and a $50,000 settlement. Never price per request, because the state controls the volume.

The regulatory calendar sets the launch sequence. In **Q4 2026**, ship a sandbox-validated MVP (CSV and Postgres plus the conformance checker), with a free conformance test as the lead magnet for the roughly 70–75% of brokers who started late. Campaign through the **January 2027** registration window. Add warehouse connectors, notice tracking and SOC 2 Type I in **Q1–Q2 2027**, and ship metrics reporting **before July 1, 2027**. In **H2 2027**, align the evidence module with the final audit rules and recruit auditors as a sales channel, then add Connecticut in **2028**.

Law firms that published DROP guidance (Kelley Drye, Troutman, Freshfields and WSGR among them) and boutiques such as In-House Privacy are natural referral partners ([Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/getting-ready-to-use-the-drop); [In-House Privacy](https://www.inhouseprivacy.com/blog/california-drop-faq)). Build the scheduler for a 30-day cadence as well. AB 883 and SB 1106 would shorten the cycle, and their fate after the September 30 signing deadline is unknown ([PUBINFO mirror](https://github.com/aaronjuar-ez/PUBINFO-2025)).

## Idea 2: scraped reposts stopped creating liability in 2025

As of October 9, 2026, **14 states plus DC require a pay range in the job posting itself**: CA, CO, CT (from October 1), HI, IL, ME, MD, MA, MN, NJ, NY, VT, VA and WA. Delaware follows on September 26, 2027, and Columbus, Ohio, starts enforcing on January 1, 2027 ([Loio tracker](https://loio.com/guides/pay-transparency-laws-by-state/); [Berkshire Associates](https://www.berkshireassociates.com/blog/delaware-pay-transparency-updates-to-go-into-effect-in-2027); [Hahn Loeser](https://www.hahnlaw.com/insights/columbus-joins-other-ohio-cities-in-adopting-pay-transparency-ordinance/)).

Litigation risk concentrates in the three states that give applicants a clear private right to money damages. **Washington** has allowed a sliding scale of $100–5,000 per violation since July 27, 2025 ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/05/washington-state-clarifies-its-pay-transparency-law)). **Virginia** has allowed actual damages since July 1, 2026, with a 15-business-day cure if the employer corrects the posting "on the original posting locations" ([Epstein Becker Green](https://epstein.sitepilot11.firmseek.com/workforce-bulletin/virginia-pay-transparency-requirements-take-effect-july-1-2026)). **Connecticut** has allowed compensatory damages plus attorney fees since October 1, 2026, with a two-year limitations period and no cure period found ([Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/public-act-no-26-12-gamechanger-connecticut-workplace-compliance-here-are)).

Elsewhere enforcement runs through agencies. Illinois fines up to $500 with a 14-day cure for a first offense, New York State charges $1,000–3,000 per violation, and California's Labor Commissioner can impose $100–10,000 ([IDOL](https://labor.illinois.gov/pay); [ADP](https://www.adp.com/spark/articles/2023/04/4-potential-consequences-of-violating-pay-transparency-laws.aspx); [DLSE](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/Pay-Trans-Instructions.pdf)).

### The liability premise and the lawsuit count are out of date

| Pitch claim | What the research found |
|---|---|
| Employers are liable for every scraped copy on aggregators | Mostly false since mid-2025. Washington's SSB 5408 (effective July 27, 2025) excludes postings "digitally replicated and published without an employer's consent" ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/05/washington-state-clarifies-its-pay-transparency-law)). Colorado's guidance says an employer is not in violation when a third party reproduces its compliant post without the pay details ([CDLE INFO #9A](https://cdle.colorado.gov/sites/cdle/files/info_%239a_transparency_in_pay_and_job_opportunities_the_colorado_epewa_part_2_05.29.24.pdf)). Illinois says job boards that scrape postings are not covered ([IDOL FAQ](https://labor.illinois.gov/faqs/equal-pay-act-salary-transparency-faq.html)), and New York's proposed rules excuse scraped ads ([Jackson Lewis](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/new-york-issues-guidance-proposes-regulations-newly-effective-pay-transparency-requirements)). Employers do stay liable for copies they *engage* a third party to publish |
| About 250 employers sued, about $0.5B in exposure | An employer-lobby figure. The Association of Washington Business said a handful of firms filed "more than 250" class actions, one Seattle firm filed 75% of them, and called half a billion dollars "potential liability" as of August 2024, not money paid ([AWB](https://www.awb.org/was-pay-transparency-law-undergoes-key-fixes-but-challenges-remain/)). Later counts range from "well over 200" ([IADC](https://www.iadclaw.org/defensecounseljournal/washington-state-pay-transparency-class-actions-a-wild-ride/)) to an uncited "300+" ([Clockspot](https://www.clockspot.com/articles/pay-transparency-laws-by-state)) |
| $5,000 statutory damages per violation | Now a $100–5,000 sliding scale, plus a 5-business-day cure for postings made through July 27, 2027 ([Epstein Becker Green](https://www.workforcebulletin.com/pay-transparency-developments-washington-limits-its-equal-pay-opportunities-act-in-2025-amendments); [Trusaic](https://trusaic.com/blog/washington-state-updates-pay-transparency-law-with-sb-5408/)) |
| 16 states plus DC have pay-transparency laws | That count mixes on-request laws (NV, RI and Connecticut before October) with posting mandates. Posting mandates number 14 states plus DC after October 1, 2026 ([Loio](https://loio.com/guides/pay-transparency-laws-by-state/)) |

Washington's lawsuit wave was real, but it is now mostly a tail of settlements over postings made before the 2025 amendment. Through 2026 these typically ran **$0.4M–4.75M per case**: Ardagh Glass at $404,795–602,000, Blue Origin at $1.96M, Scribd at $3M and Hermanson at $4.75M ([ClaimDepot, Ardagh](https://www.claimdepot.com/settlements/epoa-settlement-agi); [ClaimDepot, Blue Origin](https://www.claimdepot.com/settlements/epoa-settlement-boi); [ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/news/category/washington-equal-pay-and-opportunities-act); [ClaimDepot, Hermanson](https://www.claimdepot.com/settlements/epoa-settlement-hc-llp)).

Two developments keep the stakes high. In *Branson* (September 2025), the Washington Supreme Court held that an applicant need not be "bona fide" to recover; the plaintiffs there had applied through Indeed ([K&L Gates](https://www.klgates.com/Washington-Supreme-Court-Adopts-Expansive-Definition-of-Job-Applicant-Under-EPOA-Pay-Transparency-Statute-9-8-2025)). And employment-practices insurance coverage is contested, with some carriers reclassifying these suits as wage-and-hour claims that carry defense-only sublimits ([HUB International](https://www.hubinternational.com/products/proex/the-advocate/2026/08/pay-transparency-laws-employer-risks-and-epl-insurance-coverage/)). The pool of potential violations is wide: only **49% of US Indeed postings showed pay in late June 2026**, and that share has been flat for about a year ([Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/23/us-labor-market-snapshot-june-2026/)).

### Pre-publish checkers are crowded; live-copy monitoring is thin

The draft and own-site layer is crowded. **Ongig** rescans job descriptions hourly for missing salary ([Ongig](https://blog.ongig.com/hr-content/salary-transparency/)). **Datapeople**, now carrying a Payscale banner, flags missing pay in its editor before publishing ([Datapeople](https://help.datapeople.io/article/202-pay-transparency-and-salary-disclosure)). **SixFifty** runs a pre-publish check and pushes salary data through to Indeed and LinkedIn ([SixFifty](https://www.sixfifty.com/blog/how-to-update-job-postings-for-pay-transparency-laws/)). **Trusaic and Syndio** cover range-setting and pay reporting, but neither showed live-posting scanning ([Trusaic](https://trusaic.com/regulatory-pay-transparency-reporting/); [Syndio](https://synd.io/blog/workplace-equity-platform-for-transparency-era/)).

Monitoring of live copies is thin and cheap. An Apify community "Pay Transparency Checker" charges **$5 per 1,000 postings** ([Apify](https://apify.com/jtpalms/pay-transparency-checker)), and generic page monitors such as ChangeTower track job pages for competitor intelligence ([ChangeTower](https://changetower.com/solutions/job-posting-monitoring/)). No vendor was found offering correction or takedown workflows to aggregators.

Detecting a missing range is therefore a commodity. The open space is copy provenance plus cure-deadline workflow. The most likely fast followers already control the source data or the distribution feeds: Payscale/Datapeople, Ongig, SixFifty, and programmatic advertisers such as Joveo, which already tells employers to treat the ATS as the single source of truth ([Joveo](https://www.joveo.com/blog/job-posting-compliance-checklist/)). Pave, Pequity, Textio, Workday, iCIMS and Appcast were never checked.

### Settlements justify spend, but new Washington filings should fall

The economics favor a multi-state employer with 500+ employees, heavy remote or hourly hiring, and exposure in Washington, Virginia or Connecticut. Against $0.4–4.75M settlements, contested insurance and *Branson*'s broad standing rule, a tool costing $20,000–50,000 a year is cheap insurance. Practitioner advice reads like the product's feature list: put a designated notice address in postings, pre-identify contacts at third-party posting vendors, and keep captures of postings exactly as published ([SHRM](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/employment-law-compliance/washington-updates-guidance-pay-transparency-rules); [Stokes Lawrence](https://www.stokeslaw.com/news-insights/a-new-guide-for-complying-with-washingtons-pay-transparency-law/)).

Three problems weigh against it. The cure window, the sliding damages scale and the scraped-copy carve-out should cut new Washington filings on postings made after July 2025, so demand will likely dip through mid-2027. No evidence shows that defense firms or insurers buy scanning tools. And the legal regime has already swung once and can swing again: Washington's cure covers only postings made through July 27, 2027, after which uncured violations are exposed again, and Connecticut has no cure at all. Both point to renewed litigation in late 2027. Federal preemption risk is low, since the proposed federal Salary Transparency Act (H.R. 1599) never left committee ([Loio](https://loio.com/guides/pay-transparency-laws-by-state/)).

### Cheap data, hard jurisdiction logic

Data acquisition is inexpensive. Greenhouse's job board API returns structured pay ranges (`pay_input_ranges`) with no authentication ([Greenhouse docs](https://github.com/grnhse/greenhouse-api-docs)). Lever's public postings API exposes `salaryRange` and `workplaceType` and states that published postings "may be scraped by third parties" ([Lever](https://github.com/lever/postings-api)). Licensed feeds for finding copies cost little. TheirStack starts at $59 a month for 1,500 credits and falls to about $0.0015 per job at its top tier ([TheirStack](https://theirstack.com/de/pricing)). Techmap runs about $1 per 1,000 postings ([TheirStack comparison](https://theirstack.com/en/comparisons/theirstack-vs-techmap)). DataForSEO's Google Jobs results cost $0.0006 per page ([DataForSEO](https://dataforseo.com/pricing/serp/google-jobs-serp-api)). The open-source JobSpy scraper works for a prototype, but its README warns that "Google Jobs is currently unavailable" and that every board blocks aggressively ([JobSpy](https://github.com/speedyapply/JobSpy)). Logged-off scraping of public pages is defensible under *Meta v. Bright Data* ([Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/news-events/client-alert-what-does-the-meta-v-bright-data-summary-judgment-ruling-mean-for-web-scraping/)), so the product should never log in.

The hard part is the rules engine, because coverage of remote roles often cannot be read from the posting. California covers any role "that could be filled" by a worker in California ([California Employment Law Report](https://www.californiaemploymentlawreport.com/2026/04/what-california-law-requires-in-your-job-postings/)). Illinois, New York and Connecticut add tests based on where the role reports ([Seyfarth](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/illinois-department-of-labor-provides-long-awaited-transparency-to-equal-pay-act-amendments.html)), and Massachusetts uses "primary place of work" ([Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2226/2025-09-30-massachusetts-pay-transparency-law-key-updates-regarding)). The engine therefore needs employer-supplied reporting-location data plus a conservative default. It must also separate employer-provided pay from board estimates, using schema.org's `baseSalary` versus `estimatedSalary` distinction ([schema.org](https://github.com/schemaorg/schemaorg)).

The researchers estimate a **2–3 month MVP**. Their own estimate of data costs is about $100–600 a month for an employer with 200 open requisitions. A free public "exposure check" carries a specific risk: it hands target lists to the tester-plaintiffs that *Branson* empowered, so full results should require company-domain verification.

### Reshape as a cure-clock and provenance tool sold through defense counsel

The evidence moves idea 2 from "find every copy and take it down" to a **posting cure-clock and distribution-integrity system**. It would monitor only the copies the employer is liable for: the career site, paid boards, programmatic feeds, staffing agencies and ATS syndication. It would classify each copy as owned, authorized syndication or unauthorized scrape, with provenance evidence that supports Washington's carve-out defense. It would run business-day cure timers per jurisdiction (Washington 5, Virginia 15, Illinois 7 after notice), backed by a notice inbox and re-crawl verification. It would also check range quality and benefits text against the "good-faith" and benefits rules in Virginia, Connecticut, Illinois, Minnesota and New Jersey, and export the correction log for outside counsel.

Recruit design partners in Q4 2026 from employers already sued or sent demand letters in Washington, through their defense counsel, and lead with Connecticut and Virginia launch content. Integrate Greenhouse and Lever first, then Workday and iCIMS in H1 2027. Position the product for Columbus enforcement in January 2027, the Washington cure sunset on July 27, 2027 and Delaware in September 2027. Employment-defense firms already publish on every new state law, which makes Littler, Jackson Lewis, Ogletree, Fisher Phillips and Seyfarth the natural referral channel ([Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/relief-steep-pay-transparency-penalties-businesses-washington-state)). Insurance brokers are a second channel, though no evidence yet shows carriers want it. Price per actively monitored requisition with jurisdiction add-ons; that is a hypothesis, since no benchmark surfaced.

### Enterprise declaration platforms and $10 geo-block apps leave the middle empty

No vendor was found that combines **reusable, lab-backed component certificates shared across brands** with **legal rules pushed into checkout**. Four groups surround that gap.

**Enterprise supplier-declaration platforms** sell to compliance teams at large manufacturers on custom quotes ([Supply Chain Dive](https://www.supplychaindive.com/press-release/20250915-assent-uncovers-over-695-unique-pfas-across-global-supply-chains-as-regulat); [Assent pricing](https://www.assent.com/?p=49947); [UL](https://www.ul.com/services/proactive-pfas-management-products-across-your-supply-chain); [Certivo](https://www.certivo.com/blog-details/multi-state-pfas-reporting-reusing-data-across-minnesota-maine-and-more)). Assent claims 4.5M supplier declarations and charges suppliers nothing. 3E maintains a US PFAS rules library. UL's WERCSmart already collects PFAS documentation for large retailers, a natural network position. Certivo maps "a single PFAS dataset at product and component level" to each state. Source Intelligence and osapiens also compete here.

**Testing and certification bodies** sell PFAS-free marks. Intertek launched a PFAS-Free certification in May 2025 that requires total organic fluorine below 20 ppm, and SGS's "No PFAS Detected" mark tests a selected list of compounds and excludes fluoropolymers ([Intertek](https://w3prep.intertek.com/news/2025/intertek-launches-pfas-free-certification-program); [SGS](https://www.sgs.com/en/services/sgs-green-mark)).

**Industry data networks** already handle cross-brand sharing in places. The ZDHC Gateway lets textile suppliers control what they share with each connected brand ([ZDHC](https://knowledge-base.roadmaptozero.com/hc/en-gb/articles/37973711903517-How-do-I-control-what-data-I-share-with-connected-organisations-Suppliers)).

**Shopify geo-restriction apps** enforce only rules the merchant types in and carry no PFAS legal content. Compliant Commerce AI starts at $49.99 a month, ZIP Lock at $9.99 (Shopify Plus stores only) and Ship Restrict at $19.99 ([Compliant Commerce](https://apps.shopify.com/compliant-commerce); [ZIP Lock](https://apps.shopify.com/zip-lock); [Ship Restrict](https://apps.shopify.com/ship-restrict)).

No 2025–26 funding round for a PFAS compliance-software startup surfaced; venture money is flowing to testing and destruction technology instead ([VCA Online](https://www.vcaonline.com/news/archive/2026/)). The biggest threats are players that already hold a network: UL WERCSmart, the testing labs, ZDHC, and Certivo or Assent if either adds a checkout API.

### Penalties and class actions create demand; delays and liability limit it

The market is real but unmeasured. The only firm count is Minnesota's PRISM reporting portal, where **more than 700 companies registered** ahead of the September 15 deadline ([MPCA](https://www.pca.state.mn.us/news-and-stories/groundbreaking-reports-on-pfas-in-products-are-due-three-months-from-today)). Those are mostly industrial manufacturers whose products *contain* PFAS, not the PFAS-free brands this product would serve. Testing-market reports ($199M for the US in 2025) are dominated by water and environmental testing and do not measure software budgets ([DataM Intelligence](https://www.datamintelligence.com/research-report/u-s-pfas-testing-market)).

Testing prices set the reuse economics. A total organic fluorine test costs **€250–350 per sample** plus a €97 order fee at Measurlabs ([Measurlabs](https://measurlabs.com/products/total-organic-fluorine-tof-content-in-paper)), NZ$1,100 for one sample falling to NZ$295 above 30 at Scion ([Scion](https://www.scionresearch.com/services/laboratory-services/pfas-testing-services)), and targeted LC-MS/MS confirmation runs $400–700 ([HQTS](https://www.hqts.com/?p=183995)). A €300 test on a mill's fabric shared by 20 brands costs about €15 each, but the larger saving is avoiding repeat tests across hundreds of SKUs and components.

Willingness to pay is strongest among mid-market direct-to-consumer brands in apparel, outdoor, cookware, beauty and children's goods that cannot afford Assent, and among marketplaces that must collect certificates from sellers. Researcher price hypotheses are $300–1,500 a month for brands, $1,000–5,000 a year for a supplier "verified" tier, and $49–299 a month for a geo-fence app.

Four risks limit the business. Liability stays with the brand: under the closest legal analog, CPSC's component-part testing rule, the finished-product certifier must still attest to its own "due care" ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Component-Part-Testing)). California's DTSC will set accepted test methods and lab accreditations only by January 1, 2029 ([BCLP](https://www.bclplaw.com/en-US/events-insights-news/pfas-update-california-enacts-new-pfas-enforcement-and-registration-law.html)), so today's certificates might not match tomorrow's rules. Repeated delays let buyers defer purchases. And no evidence surfaced on whether labs will allow their reports to be shared with third parties, which the researchers call **the single biggest feasibility question** for the network.

### Component testing has legal precedent; lab sharing rights do not

The evidence model is workable. States that ban "intentionally added" PFAS (ME, MN, NY, CT, NM) generally require no routine testing. There, a signed supplier declaration is the primary proof, backed by "test results or other supporting evidence" if an agency asks. The fluorine thresholds work differently: California and Vermont at 50 ppm from 2027, and Washington's 50 ppm presumption, make a test result the de facto proof. A **total organic fluorine result at or below 50 ppm for each homogeneous component**, with a detection limit of 10 ppm or better, clears every numeric threshold found.

Component testing is also the conservative choice. Law-firm summaries say California's threshold applies to "the product or product components" ([Morgan Lewis](https://morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/11/new-york-and-california-bans-on-pfas-in-textiles-and-apparel-begin-january-1-2025)), and testing a whole garment can dilute a fluorinated trim below the limit. Two precedents support reuse. CPSC's component-part testing rule lets finished-product certifiers rely on component test reports when its conditions are met ([16 CFR 1109.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/1109.5)). OEKO-TEX's modular system recognizes pre-certified yarns, fabrics and trims downstream to "avoid double testing" ([Hohenstein](https://www.hohenstein.de/en-us/oeko-tex/modular-system)).

YKK's 2023 discovery that a paint supplier had added PFAS to zipper-hardware enamel shows why each certificate needs lot or version scoping and an expiry date ([GearJunkie](https://gearjunkie.com/news/pfas-outdoor-gear-pfc-ykk-zippers)). The platform should never issue its own "PFAS-free" mark; even OEKO-TEX declines to certify products as PFAS-free ([Hohenstein](https://www.hohenstein.de/en-us/oeko-tex/restrictions-and-testing/pfas)).

On the commerce side, the right Shopify building block is a server-side Cart and Checkout Validation Function. Public apps built on Functions work on Basic plans and above, while custom apps need Plus ([Shopify.dev](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/build/checkout/cart-checkout-validation); [Shopify Help](https://help.shopify.com/manual/checkout-settings/checkout-customization)). The researchers estimate **6–10 engineer-weeks** for a Shopify MVP. The rules engine needs three outcomes (allow, allow-with-disclosure, block) across roughly 12 states and 15 product categories. The full network is the largest build of the four ideas.

### Start with rules and checkout, then seed the network from partners' bills of materials

The evidence reshapes idea 4 into a **rules-first, network-second** product, built in three layers.

The first layer works with zero network data: a maintained state rules engine plus checkout validation and an evidence vault. Its first buyers are merchants who still sell PFAS-containing products nationally, such as PTFE-coated cookware, ski wax, fabric treatments, and outerwear for severe wet conditions during its exemption windows. For them, blocking sales by state saves revenue rather than only reducing risk.

The second layer is an attestation vault. It stores supplier declarations and lab reports and generates the brand's own certificates of compliance (for California, and for Maine and Minnesota on request) and its state reports.

The third layer is the certificate network. Seed it by asking 3–5 design-partner outdoor brands on Shopify, with $10–100M in revenue, for their top 20 materials. Then recruit those suppliers with a free listing and batched, subsidized tests: membrane makers like Gore and Polartec, trim makers like YKK, water-repellent finish (DWR) makers, and the Taiwan and Vietnam mills behind them ([Polartec/Milliken](https://www.milliken.com/en-us/textiles/blogs/polartec-goes-non-pfas-in-durable-water-repellent-treatments); [YKK DynaPel](https://www.textileworld.com/textile-world/knitting-apparel/2023/11/ykk-launches-new-water-repellant-zipper-designed-to-work-in-circular-systems-new-dynapel-zipper-uses-gtt-empel-technology)).

Interview two or three labs about report-sharing terms before writing any network code. A "report-to-network" API that gives labs volume in exchange is the natural channel. The January 1, 2027 deadline is twelve weeks away, too close to sell a network against. The realistic targets are Washington's first reports on January 31, 2027 and the January 2028 wave: Connecticut's ban, the end of California's and New York's exemption for severe-wet-weather outerwear, New Mexico's second wave and New Jersey. Retailers come later, once the network has enough suppliers, as a channel that requires their vendors to onboard.

## Idea 5: EPR fee math is free; converter data plumbing is not

Seven states have packaging extended producer responsibility (EPR) laws: Maine, Oregon, Colorado, California, Minnesota, Maryland and Washington. None was added in 2026 ([SPC](https://sustainablepackaging.org/2026/09/23/packaging-policy-news/)). Only two invoice program fees today. Oregon has invoiced since 2025 and published its 2027 schedule on October 1, 2026 ([CAA](https://circularactionalliance.org/or-2027-producer-fees)); Colorado has invoiced since January 2026 ([Baker Donelson](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/countdown-to-compliance-packaging-epr-deadlines)).

California charged a one-time "Early Fee" of just 0.3–2.5¢ per pound in August 2026 ([EPR Atlas](https://epratlas.com/updates/2026-08-03-caa-publishes-california-s-2026-early-fee-schedule-six/)). Its full program fees begin once CalRecycle approves the plan of the Circular Action Alliance (CAA), the producer responsibility organization; approval is targeted for January 1, 2027. The program carries a projected **$1.26–1.87B budget plus a $500M-a-year plastics surcharge** ([EPR Atlas](https://epratlas.com/california/); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/californias-epr-program-plan)). Maryland fees begin in 2028, Minnesota in 2029 and Washington around 2030. Maine is stalled after its request for a program operator reportedly drew no bidders ([CSG Midwest](https://csgmidwest.org/2026/03/11/most-states-have-some-type-of-extended-producer-responsibility-statute-minnesota-is-implementing-one-new-to-the-midwest-epr-for-packaging/); [WA Ecology](https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/2507046.pdf); [Resource Recycling](https://resource-recycling.com/policy-now/2026/08/20/no-bidders-respond-to-maines-epr-rfp/)).

### Free calculators, earlier fees and an Oregon court win change the pitch

| Pitch claim | What the research found |
|---|---|
| A fee simulator is a new product | At least six free calculators exist. EPR Atlas prices a packaging bill of materials across 7 states and compares redesigns side by side; EPR Fee Check models a second material-shift scenario; rePurpose, LAPPA, extended-producer-responsibility.com and PackEasy also estimate fees; and suppliers such as Paper Tube Co. run their own calculators as sales tools ([EPR Atlas](https://epratlas.com/unit-calculator/); [EPR Fee Check](https://eprfeecheck.com/); [rePurpose](https://www.repurpose.global/blog/how-to-estimate-your-2026-epr-fees-introducing-repurposes-new-epr-fee-calculator); [LAPPA](https://lappa.org/epr-fee-calculator/); [Paper Tube Co.](https://papertube.co/blogs/down-the-tubes/epr-packaging-fees-calculation)) |

| Oregon's and Colorado's laws are under court challenge | Oregon's law was **upheld in full** on August 27–28, 2026 and is on appeal to the 9th Circuit (No. 26-6404) ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/federal-court-upholds-oregons-epr-law-in-full-after-constitutional); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/ninth-circuit-review-constitutional-challenge-oregons-epr-law)). Rates vary sharply by material and swing year to year. Oregon's 2026 rates ran from 8¢ per pound for corrugated to 43¢ for PE film and 102¢ for PP film ([CAA 2026 schedule](https://circularactionalliance.org/s/OR-2026-Fee-Schedule-Public.pdf); [Foresight](https://www.useforesight.io/news/oregon-2027-packaging-epr-fee-schedule)). In 2027 those film rates fell to 12¢ and 56¢, partly because about $80M of program reserves is being returned through lower rates ([CAA 2027 schedule](https://circularactionalliance.org/s/OR-2027FeeSchedule_100126.pdf)). California's preliminary, non-binding 2027 structure adds **26¢ per pound for the plastics mitigation fund**, 3¢ for reuse investment and 0.1¢ per plastic component on top of base rates ([EPR Atlas](https://epratlas.com/california/)). Those gaps are what a "swap material X for Y" simulator sells, and the volatility is what makes it hard to keep accurate.

### Specright, Lorax and six free calculators already own the arithmetic

The brand-side enterprise stack is mature. Specright, a packaging-specification platform, generates multi-state EPR reports "via Lorax" ([Specright](https://www.specright.com/one-pager/epr-compliance-with-specright-and-lorax-epi/)). It also hosts the Sustainable Packaging Data Council, whose members include Costco, Tyson, Sonoco, Atlantic Packaging, Lorax EPI, Trayak and How2Recycle; the council published free reporting templates that set out which data brands own and which suppliers own ([Specright press release](https://specright.com/press-releases/sustainable-packaging-data-council-forms-epr-reporting-template)). Assent sells packaging EPR modules in partnership with Lorax EPI ([Verdantix](https://www.verdantix.com/client-portal/blog/from-burden-to-strategy--mastering-packaging-epr-compliance)).

Scenario and redesign tools are multiplying. **PCX Markets** advertises fee projections and allocation by SKU ([PCX](https://www.pcxmarkets.com/epr-packaging-compliance)), and **Packgine** calculates current and projected fees for every SKU ([Packgine](https://packgine.ai/epr)). **Planet FWD** sells help qualifying for Oregon's fee bonuses, which are based on life-cycle assessments ([Planet FWD](https://www.planetfwd.com/blog/blog-oregon-epr-eco-modulation-bonus-a-b-c)). **rePurpose** lists component optimization as "coming soon," and self-serve tools such as Repax start at €0–29 a month ([Repax](https://www.repax.io/blog/best-epr-software)).

No vendor was found selling a white-label simulator that converters hand to their brand customers. That is the gap the idea targets. But the converter ERP vendor ePS already runs a "Radius EPR Solutions" landing page ([ePS](https://go.epackagingsw.com/Packaging_LP_Radius_EPR_Solutions)), so ERP vendors are circling. The most dangerous entrants are Specright, which already sits between brands and suppliers, rePurpose, which is already courting suppliers, and the ERP vendors. Lorax EPI's product detail and most large converters' own tools were never checked.

### A real fee pool, an unproven payer

The money is real; the payer is not. The buyer pool is concentrated. US flexible packaging was **$42.6B in 2024 across about 600 converters, with the top 25 holding 54% of revenue** ([FPA](https://www.flexpack.org/news/fpa-publishes-2025-state-of-the-us-flexible-packaging-industry-report); [Converting Quarterly](https://convertingquarterly.com/?p=8790)), and Label Traxx alone claims about 500 converter customers on its ERP ([Amtech](https://www.amtechsoftware.com/solutions/label-traxx-erp/)). The largest converters will build in-house or plug into Specright. That leaves a long tail of a few hundred mid-size flexible, label and paperboard converters, a niche worth single-digit millions in ARR unless brands also pay.

Converters clearly feel pulled into an EPR data role; Sonoco's sustainability VP calls that role "central" ([Packaging World](https://www.packworld.com/sustainable-packaging/recycling/article/22952828/brands-and-converters-align-on-epr-data-demands)). Yet **no evidence was found that any converter pays for an EPR tool for its customers**. A converter selling non-recyclable laminates has reason to resist a tool that makes its product look expensive.

Savings are small outside California. Cents per pound times Oregon and Colorado volumes is modest for mid-size brands, so California is what makes swaps material from 2027. NAW's Colorado complaint says that law bars businesses from disclosing government-mandated fees to customers ([PlasticsToday](https://www.plasticstoday.com/legislation-regulations/naw-sues-colorado-over-plastic-packaging-epr-law)), so an "EPR cost" line on a converter's quote needs legal review there. Repeal risk is low after Oregon's merits win. A 9th Circuit reversal or a Colorado injunction would mainly exempt NAW's distributor members rather than end the programs.

### Category mapping and annual PDF transcription are the real build

The fee formula is trivial: the sum of each component's weight times its state rate, adjusted for eco-modulation bonuses and penalties. The work is in the data. Converters hold the per-unit half: layer structure, gauge and weight, recycled content, inks and adhesives, and dimensions. Brands hold the state-by-state unit volumes and the components bought from other suppliers. A converter alone can therefore produce a credible "**fee per 1,000 units sold into state X**." A total bill requires either brand input or an apportionment assumption that CAA has not been confirmed to accept.

Classification must be exact. When different materials cannot be separated, the whole item is reported at full weight in the category of whichever material makes up most of that weight. Packaging with two or more sides of 2 inches or less goes into a small-format category ([CAA definitions](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/64260ed078c36925b1cf3385/t/683dfa7823960e3fa03725bc/1748892281594/Covered+Materials+%26+Producer+Definitions+%28OR%26CO%29+-+June+2025.pdf)). California also needs a count of plastic components per unit ([California Grocers Assn.](https://www.cagrocers.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/SB-54-Producer-Reporting-Requirements-5.4.26.pdf)). CAA publishes schedules only as PDFs, with no API or CSV, so rates must be transcribed every October and whenever mid-year changes appear. The researchers estimate 2–4 analyst-days per state per release.

Recyclability output carries extra legal risk. California's SB 343 labeling rules apply to products manufactured after October 4, 2026, though a July 14, 2026 preliminary injunction blocks enforcement to an extent sources describe differently ([CalRecycle](https://calrecycle.ca.gov/wcs/recyclinglabels/); [Waste Dive](https://www.wastedive.com/news/navigating-california-truth-in-labeling-sb-343/832396/)). The tool should report each list's status with its source and never generate a "recyclable" claim.

The researchers estimate a **3–4 month MVP for one or two engineers plus a part-time EPR analyst**. ERP connectors would follow at 4–8 weeks per system, starting with Label Traxx's documented API.

### Recast as a converter data pack sold through ERP vendors, or skip it

The evidence turns idea 5 from a "fee simulator" into a **converter EPR data pack**. Its core is per-SKU component data exported in CAA's report-workbook format and the Specright council templates, so it drops straight into the brand's reporting. On top of that sit per-1,000-unit fee and swap comparisons by state, with the rate-table version and preliminary-or-final status stamped on every number. California appears as low–high scenario bands until CalRecycle approves the plan. The pack also flags redesigns that could earn an Oregon life-cycle-assessment bonus, and it ships as a white-labeled report the converter sends to each brand customer.

Sell it as a sales-enablement tool that wins or defends RFQs, not as compliance software. The most efficient route to market is OEM distribution through converter ERP vendors (Label Traxx, Radius, Kiwiplan), which already hold the specification data. Veritiv, which advertises "EPR guidance" services, is the most plausible distributor partner or competitor ([Veritiv](https://www.veritiv.com/home)).

The selling window runs from Q4 2026 through Q2 2027. It is anchored by the October 1 publication of 2027 rates, California's plan approval around January 1, Oregon and Colorado invoices in January and July, and the next supply reports around May 31, 2027. The deciding step comes before any code: ten discovery calls with mid-size flexible and label converters. If none will pay, drop the idea or offer it to an ERP vendor as an integration.

## Conclusion

Each pitch's central premise went out of date within months. Washington legislated away scraped-copy liability, a $99 DROP filer reached the market before the idea did, free calculators absorbed the EPR arithmetic, and PFAS state counts and deadlines kept moving. What survived in all four is the same asset: **a versioned record of what each rule said on a given date, plus evidence of what the company did about it**. That is the DROP audit pack, the pay-transparency cure log, the PFAS due-care file and the EPR estimate stamped with its rate-table version. Detection and arithmetic sell for $5 per 1,000 postings or $99 a month; evidence that holds up for an auditor, a cure deadline or a due-care inquiry sells for thousands. So the real choice among these ideas is which evidence trail has the nearest *mandatory* reader. DROP's auditor arrives in 2028 with a look-back to August 2026, while the other three still depend on plaintiffs, agencies or customers choosing to ask.

The next nine months concentrate the selling moments across all four markets. January 2027 brings DROP registration at $9,500, California's 50 ppm PFAS textile limit and Washington's bans, Oregon and Colorado EPR invoices, and California's EPR plan approval. July 2027 brings the DROP metrics deadline and the Washington cure sunset. That timing favors ideas that can ship in a quarter over ideas that need a network to form first. And because this research rests on search summaries, the first week of effort should go to the verification list above, not to code.
